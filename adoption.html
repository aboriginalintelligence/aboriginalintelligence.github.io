How a draft becomes law

Nothing on this site is law. A code becomes law only when the electors vote it in by secret ballot. A code council adopts by itself can be undone by council by itself.

This is a general guide, not legal advice.Confirm the steps with a lawyer before calling a vote.

The steps

  1. Check the rule already in force

    The election code or policy in force usually says how it can be changed. That rule has to be met as well. Copy its amendment section word for word before planning anything.

  2. Finish the draft and get it reviewed

    A lawyer checks it against the Indian Act, the Criminal Code, the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, and other federal law.

  3. Put it in front of every member

    Post the full text and a plain summary. Hold sessions on reserve and online, and record them. Leave at least 60 days for written comments, then post what was changed because of them.

  4. Call the vote properly

    A council resolution at a proper meeting, or a member petition where the rules allow one, sets the date, the exact question, and the budget for the vote.

  5. Use an independent electoral officer

    Someone with no family, job, or money tie to council or the First Nation runs the voters list, the ballots, and the count, and can't be removed during the vote.

  6. Reach every elector

    Every elector, on and off reserve. The voters list is posted for corrections. The notice package goes out at least 45 days before the vote, with mail ballots for anyone who wants one.

  7. Vote and count

    Secret ballot. One question per code. Scrutineers can watch the count. The count is posted within 3 days with a signed certificate.

  8. Make it official and keep the record

    Post the adopted text. Send any notice the law requires. Keep every list, notice, ballot, and certificate for at least 10 years.

How many votes it takes

What is being decidedWhat it takes
Adopt a code, or make an ordinary changeMore yes than no, and at least 25 percent of all electors voting
Remove or weaken a protected ruleAt least two thirds yes, and at least 40 percent of all electors voting
Take control of membership, or change membership rulesA majority of all electors, as Indian Act section 10 requires
Remove a chief or councillorA petition signed by 40 percent of electors, then more yes than no with at least 25 percent of electors voting

Protected rules are listed in the constitution, Part 8. They include the ban on voting on your own pay, the spending limits, the posting of financial statements, the fixed election day, and the independence of the appeal body.

Deadlines that matter

Time limitWhat it applies to
30 daysAsking the Federal Court to review a decision of a band council, electoral officer, or appeal body. Federal Courts Act, subsection 18.1(2).
Day of publicationA by-law made under the Indian Act comes into force the day it is first published, or a later day it names. Indian Act, subsection 86(4).
120 days after year endAudited financial statements and the pay and expenses schedule must be on the First Nation's website. First Nations Financial Transparency Act, section 8.

What can't happen

  • Council can't adopt, change, or suspend a code by resolution.
  • No single person can stop a vote that was properly called. Only a court order made on notice can. A court filing on its own does not.
  • Several unrelated changes can't be bundled into one yes or no question.
  • An unadopted draft can't be used as if it were law.
